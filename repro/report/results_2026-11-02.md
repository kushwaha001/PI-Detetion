# Probe-based PI detection under domain shift — results

Oct 29, 2026 · @Kanishk

## Summary

Hidden-state probes detect indirect prompt injection far better than an off-the-shelf classifier, but they only survive a domain change if their layer is chosen without in-domain validation and their threshold is re-set on the new domain.

1. **PIShield's way of choosing the layer fails under shift.** Validation accuracy saturates on the earliest layers, so it picks layers 2–7 and loses 0.18–0.20 AUROC on average across 4 models, up to 0.44.
2. **A label-free intrinsic-dimension rule is the safest layer choice.** Its average loss against the best layer is 0.021 on BIPIA and 0.026 on SQuAD, against 0.062 and 0.029 for a fixed mid-depth layer. It picks the best layer exactly for Qwen2.5-0.5B and 1.5B on BIPIA and is within 0.003 on SQuAD for Qwen2.5-3B, but lands 3–7 layers early on TinyLlama and 2–6 layers late on Qwen2.5-7B, where a mid-depth layer does better.
3. **Thresholds collapse in new domains.** A 5% threshold from metro flags 61–86% of benign documents elsewhere. Fifty clean target documents bring that back to 1–6%.
4. **Recalibration can be poisoned.** Decoys in 5% of the calibration traffic drive every quantile threshold to 0% detection. A median/MAD transfer rule keeps 34–45% detection with 20% decoys on three of four models.
5. **Single-layer probes are easy to evade, all layers together are not.** A white-box suffix drops the attacked layer's detection from 99% to 7% while the injection still works, but layers 18–24 still catch 80–99%.
6. **Hard negatives remain the open problem.** The DeBERTa classifier is below chance on BIPIA (AUROC 0.28–0.47) and flags 69% of NotInject; probes flag 10–56%, and training on benign-instruction negatives does not reliably help.

The defensible claims are rows 1, 5, 6, 9 and 12: validation-based layer choice fails under shift, label-free ID choice is safe, thresholds must be re-set per domain and can be poisoned, and single-layer probes are evadable while deep layers are not. Rows 7, 8 and 10 are negative results worth one sentence each in a paper.

## Setup

Every probe is a logistic regression (C = 0.2) on one layer's hidden state of a frozen model, trained only on the Latent Drift Guard metro data, then tested on domains it never saw.

| Item | What was used |
| --- | --- |
| Models | Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (28 layers), Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (24), TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat (22), OLMo-2-1B-Instruct (16); fp16 or bf16 on a 4 GB RTX 2050 |
| Training data | Pune Metro manual chunks with injected attacks: A blatant override, B smooth fixed payload, C subtle task redirect; benign; D benign documents with reader-directed instructions. Probe trained on half the metro test documents, seen tasks only |
| Shift 1 | SQuAD paragraphs with the same attack families (10,400 records in total, all layers) |
| Shift 2 | [BIPIA](https://github.com/microsoft/BIPIA) test contexts: 50 emails, 100 tables, 50 code-error threads, each clean plus 2–3 BIPIA attacks inserted at start, middle or end (700 records), plus 160 clean training contexts as unlabeled target traffic |
| Hard negatives | [NotInject](https://huggingface.co/datasets/leolee99/NotInject): 339 benign sentences full of trigger words ("ignore", "override", "roleplay") inserted into benign emails |
| Baseline | [ProtectAI DeBERTa-v3 prompt-injection classifier v2](https://huggingface.co/protectai/deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2), 512-token windows, max over windows |
| Features | Last prompt token (PIShield's read-out) and the drift delta (state with the document minus state with the task only) |
| Metrics | AUROC averaged over attack families (held-out-family probes), false-alarm rate (FPR) and detection rate (TPR) at a 5% conformal threshold, regret = best layer's AUROC minus the chosen layer's |

## Layer selection across models and benchmarks

Picking the probe layer from the intrinsic dimension (ID) of benign traffic is the safest of the rules tested: on BIPIA it loses 0.021 AUROC on average against the best layer, against 0.202 for PIShield's validation-accuracy pick.

The ID rule, frozen before TinyLlama and OLMo-2 were run (`FROZEN_RULES.md`), is: compute the TwoNN intrinsic dimension of 1,600 benign metro prompts at every layer, average 10 bootstrap subsamples, and take the first local peak whose height is within 10% of the curve's maximum. It never sees an attack. PIShield's rule trains a probe on every layer and keeps the one with the best in-domain validation accuracy.

| ID near-top peak (label-free) | 0.026 | 0.082 | 0.021 | 0.089 |
| Fixed mid-depth layer | 0.029 | 0.082 | 0.062 | 0.149 |
| Average of all layers' scores | — | — | 0.047 | 0.099 |

PIShield's rule always lands on layers 2–7, because every early layer separates the in-domain validation split almost perfectly; those layers transfer worst. On SQuAD the ID rule only ties the mid-depth default, but on BIPIA, the larger shift, it is best on both Qwen models and within 0.02 on OLMo-2. TinyLlama is the exception on both shifts: its best layers are near the top (L17–L20), and its ID peak lands 3–7 layers early. A version that requires a statistically significant peak was tried and dropped: it picked layer 1 on OLMo-2.

## Threshold collapse and target-domain recalibration

A threshold set for 5% false alarms on metro data flags 61–86% of benign documents in a new domain; re-setting it on 50–60 clean documents from that domain brings false alarms back to 1–6% on every model, with no attack labels.

Conformal thresholds assume new data looks like the calibration data, and a domain change breaks that. The fix only needs a small sample of normal traffic from the deployment domain. All numbers below are at the ID-selected layer, last-prompt-token feature.

| Model (layer) | SQuAD benign FPR, metro threshold | SQuAD FPR / TPR (family C), recalibrated | BIPIA benign FPR, metro threshold | BIPIA FPR / TPR, recalibrated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Qwen2.5-1.5B (L17) | 0.68 | 0.01 / 0.83 | 0.86 | 0.04 / 0.72 |
| Qwen2.5-0.5B (L14) | 0.61 | 0.01 / 0.65 | 0.70 | 0.03 / 0.47 |
| TinyLlama-1.1B (L13) | 0.71 | 0.02 / 0.35 | 0.73 | 0.03 / 0.20 |
| OLMo-2-1B (L11) | 0.69 | 0.06 / 0.78 | 0.78 | 0.05 / 0.41 |

SQuAD recalibration uses 50 clean documents (benign plus benign-with-instructions) from one half of the SQuAD documents and is measured on the other half, averaged over 20 draws. BIPIA recalibration uses the 50–60 clean training contexts of each domain and is measured on the test contexts; BIPIA rates are means over email, table and code. Detection after recalibration is honest but modest on BIPIA: 20–72% of attacks at about 4% false alarms. Once the threshold is fixed, layer choice matters again: on SQuAD, PIShield's pick on Qwen2.5-1.5B (L6) catches 24% of family-C attacks at the same false-alarm rate, against 83% at L17.

## Recalibrating from contaminated traffic

If an attacker can slip obvious decoys into 5% of the traffic used for recalibration, every quantile-based threshold detects 0% of attacks afterwards, on all four models; a robust median/MAD rule still detects 34–64% with 20% decoys.

The unlabeled target traffic used to set the threshold contains some attacks. Let a fraction e of the n pool scores be contaminants with unknown distribution, and let the threshold be the pool's empirical q-quantile. Because the pool's CDF is (1 − e)·F\_benign + e·F\_contam, the benign false-alarm rate is only pinned to an interval:

```latex
\frac{1-e-q}{1-e} \;\le\; \mathrm{FPR}_{\text{benign}}(t_q) \;\le\; \frac{1-q}{1-e}
```

Three consequences follow. The naive rule (q = 1 − α) can inflate to α/(1 − e) when contaminants score low. Setting q = 1 − α(1 − e\_max) guarantees FPR ≤ α for any e ≤ e\_max, whatever the attacks look like. But the interval's width, e/(1 − e), is the same for every q: no rule that reads only the pool can both guarantee its false-alarm rate and resist an attacker who places the contaminants. Once decoys exceed α of the pool, any q ≥ 1 − α puts the threshold above every benign score.

The robust affine rule sidesteps this by assuming the benign score law only shifts and rescales between domains: t = median(pool) + k · MAD(pool), with k = (q₁₋α(source) − median(source)) / MAD(source) fixed on metro calibration data. Median and MAD tolerate up to 50% contamination.

| Qwen2.5-0.5B | robust affine | 0.05 / 0.68 | 0.02 / 0.59 | 0.03 / 0.63 | 0.00 / 0.35 |

The conservative rule behaves like the naive one to within 0.01–0.04 and is left out. Real attacks in the pool make every quantile rule more conservative, not less: detection falls from 0.81 to 0.50 on Qwen2.5-1.5B at 10% contamination. The robust affine rule fails on TinyLlama (FPR 0.13 on a clean pool), where the benign score distribution changes shape between domains rather than only shifting. The naive rule's FPR of 0.15 on OLMo-2 with a clean pool shows that 200 records from about 40 documents are not exchangeable enough for the 5% guarantee to hold.

## Hard negatives, NotInject and the DeBERTa baseline

The off-the-shelf DeBERTa prompt-injection classifier scores below chance on BIPIA (AUROC 0.28–0.47) and flags 69% of NotInject's benign documents, while hidden-state probes reach 0.51–0.95; adding benign-instruction documents as training negatives does not reliably fix false alarms.

| Detector | BIPIA AUROC email / table / code | NotInject FPR, recalibrated threshold | Hard-negative training: BIPIA mean AUROC | Hard-negative training: NotInject FPR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Probe, Qwen2.5-1.5B L17 | 0.92 / 0.95 / 0.94 | 0.56 | 0.94 → 0.87 | 0.56 → 0.34 |
| Probe, Qwen2.5-0.5B L14 | 0.79 / 0.91 / 0.86 | 0.10 | 0.85 → 0.84 | 0.10 → 0.14 |
| Probe, TinyLlama-1.1B L13 | 0.64 / 0.69 / 0.51 | 0.12 | 0.61 → 0.69 | 0.12 → 0.21 |
| Probe, OLMo-2-1B L11 | 0.72 / 0.85 / 0.68 | 0.15 | 0.75 → 0.77 | 0.15 → 0.22 |
| DeBERTa-v3 PI classifier v2 | 0.47 / 0.28 / 0.41 | 0.69 (its default threshold) | — | — |

The NotInject false-alarm rate uses the threshold re-set on clean BIPIA emails, since NotInject sentences were inserted into emails. The DeBERTa classifier was trained on direct jailbreak-style prompts, and many BIPIA attacks are polite task requests ("Write a script to rename all files in a directory…", "Add the following code snippet in your response…"), so it misses them while firing on harmless trigger words. At its default threshold it catches 55% of email attacks at 62% false alarms, and almost nothing in tables or code.

Hard-negative training adds the metro family-D documents (benign text with reader-directed instructions) as negatives. It helps where the probe was over-firing most (Qwen2.5-1.5B: NotInject false alarms 56% → 34%) but costs 0.07 AUROC there, and it raises NotInject false alarms on the other three models. Separating harmless trigger words from real attacks stays hard: AUROC of attacks against NotInject documents is only 0.57–0.74 across models.

## Adaptive white-box attack

A 15-token adversarial suffix optimised against the layer-14 probe cuts that probe's detection from 99% to 7%, but probes on layers 18–24 still flag 80–99% of the same injections.

&#91;embedded content: attack\_result.json, Qwen2.5-0.5B, 120 held-out family-A injections, 2026-10-29\]

The attacker knows the model, the probe layer and its weights. It appends one universal suffix right after the injected instruction and tunes it with greedy coordinate gradient (150 steps, 8 training injections) to push the probe's score down. The suffix transfers to most shallow and middle layers, and it raises layer 2's detection to 95% because the gibberish itself looks unusual there. It does not stop the injection working: the model still emitted the payload in 35% of generations with the suffix, against 38% without. Averaging every layer's standardised score detects 40% of the suffixed injections. A single-layer probe is therefore easy to evade; two probes far apart in depth (here L14 and L22) would force the attacker to fool both, which this run did not try.

The best layers are L20–L24 on SQuAD and L21 on BIPIA, so the frozen rule's L26 sits a few layers late but on the plateau. After re-setting the threshold on 50 clean SQuAD documents, L26 catches 94% / 79% / 84% of families A / B / C at 5% false alarms on benign documents, against 17% / 14% / 16% for PIShield's L5. Two caveats: for the drift-delta feature, 5 of 10 bootstrap resamples of the ID curve picked L4–L5 instead of L26, so the rule is less stable on this model; and NotInject false alarms at L26 stay high (51–54% after recalibration).

The best layers are L13–L17, and probes there are very strong (AUROC 0.98–1.00). Two things differ from every other model. | Qwen2.5-7B | L16 (L8 close) | 1.00 | 0.94 | 0.99 | 0.995 (L13) |
| TinyLlama-1.1B | L7 | 0.86 | 0.76 | 0.75 | 0.91 (L16) |
| OLMo-2-1B | L3 | 0.68 | 0.85 | 0.83 | 0.93 (L9) |

Transfer from BIPIA is weaker than transfer from metro on every model (SQuAD 0.75–0.99 vs 0.91–0.995); BIPIA gives only 150 clean training examples. On the Qwen models the BIPIA-trained probe still works best in the middle-to-upper layers, within a few layers of where the metro-trained probe peaks, and on 7B the gap almost closes. On TinyLlama and OLMo-2 the best layer moves well down the network (L7 vs L16, L3 vs L9) and the transfer is much weaker, so the best layer depends on the training data as well as the model. In both directions the last layer transfers poorly to SQuAD and metro (0.56–0.70 on SQuAD for the BIPIA-trained probe).

### Multi-layer adaptive attack (Qwen2.5-0.5B)

Same universal-suffix attack as before, 80 steps, 120 test injections; detection at a 2.5% false-positive threshold per layer.

| Attack target | Detection at the attacked layers (clean → attacked) | Other layers under attack | OR of L14 and L22 | Payload followed (clean → attacked) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L14 alone (earlier run) | L14: 99% → 7% | L18–24 still 80–99% | — | 38% → 35% |
| L22 alone | L22: 98% → 28% | L23 48%, L24 24%; L4–20 still 98–100% | 100% | 38% → 25% |
| L14 and L22 jointly | L14: 99% → 97%; L22: 98% → 30% | L21 85%, L23–24 25–30% | 97% | 38% → 23% |

A suffix that fools one layer leaves layers far from it working, and attacking L14 and L22 together failed: within 80 steps the optimiser got past L22 but not L14, so a detector that alerts when either layer fires still caught 97%. This is evidence for combining an early-middle and a late layer, not proof of robustness: a longer or stronger attack may still evade both.

A stronger joint attack on L14 + L22 (200 steps, 25-token suffix) was started on Nov 2 but stalled on the laptop after 20 steps, with the objective falling from 34.6 to 6.4 but not yet below 0 (the point where both layers are evaded). It needs a rerun, with a 20-token suffix or fewer candidates per step to stay within GPU memory.

## Limitations and next steps

Results now cover six models (0.5–7B) and two training directions; 7B rests on a trimmed run.

- **Model scale.** 3B and 7B ran in 4-bit; 7B used two test tasks and 80 probe-training records. A full-precision 8B run (e.g. on a free Kaggle GPU) is still worth doing.
- **The ID rule does not win at 7B.** Its claim is narrowed to avoiding the large failures of PIShield's validation pick and of the last layer, not to finding the best layer.
- **Small benchmark slices.** 50–100 contexts per BIPIA domain and about 40 documents per SQuAD half; the bootstrap intervals above are the honest error bars.
- **The ID rule was proposed after seeing Qwen2.5-0.5B.** TinyLlama, OLMo-2, Qwen-3B and Qwen-7B are the held-out tests.
- **The adaptive attack covered one small model and 80–150 steps.** Longer attacks, other suffix lengths and a larger model are untested.
- **Obedience labels exist only for Qwen2.5-1.5B on metro/SQuAD.** BIPIA AUROC counts every inserted attack, whether or not the model would have followed it.

Next: finish the small-model intervals; a stronger attack on the L14 + L22 OR-detector; then a workshop paper built around threshold collapse, robust recalibration and label-free layer choice.
